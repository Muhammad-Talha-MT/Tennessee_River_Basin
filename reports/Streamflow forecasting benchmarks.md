# NWM Forecasts and Autoregressive LSTMs Anchor the Benchmark

The strongest public, reproducible benchmark suite for a 6-hourly, 10-day GNN forecaster on NHDPlus v2 reaches in the Tennessee basin rests on three pillars. The first is the **NOAA National Water Model (NWM) medium-range operational forecast archive** in `gs://national-water-model`. Its daily folders are complete from 2018-09-17 onward, it is indexed directly by NHDPlus v2 COMID, it starts from initial states that assimilate USGS observations, and it runs to 10 days. It is the only operational archive that matches the paper's reaches, horizon and test window. The second is a **regional autoregressive forecast LSTM that the authors retrain themselves** (a NeuralHydrology handoff/sequential forecast LSTM or Google's OpenHydroNet ME-LSTM). It must use the same gauges, forcing, splits and observed-flow history as the GNN. This is the strongest no-topology baseline, because adding lagged observed flow alone raises median daily CAMELS NSE from about 0.80 to 0.88. The third is **streamflow persistence, climatology and an isolated-node ablation of the GNN itself**. The ablation is essential because the most rigorous published GNN test found that topology added nothing over an MLP when observed discharge was an input. Differentiable models (δHBV2.0MTS-MC, median hourly NSE 0.683 vs 0.461 for NWM3.0) and published GNN routers are useful secondary and positioning comparators. None has been run in forecast mode with observed flow at multi-day leads, and no reach-level GNN with observed-flow inputs has been shown at about 59k reaches, so the paper's setup fills a genuine gap. GloFAS, Google Flood Hub/GRRR and NWS River Forecast Center products fail on resolution, archive coverage or independence from TVA, and belong at most in a supplementary table. No published forecast skill exists for the Tennessee basin for any of these systems. That makes a careful NWM medium-range verification for 2020-2025 a contribution in its own right.

## Information regime, not architecture, decides the ranking

Before choosing models, the paper has to separate three things every baseline varies on. The first is whether the lead window is driven by **real NWP forecasts or by observed ("perfect") forcing**. The second is whether the model **sees observed streamflow up to issue time**, through autoregression, data assimilation (DA) or post-processing. The third is the **native time step**. Published numbers show these regime differences are as large as architecture differences. On 531 CAMELS basins, a simulation LSTM reached median daily NSE **0.796**, and feeding a one-day-lagged observed flow raised it to **0.879** ([Nearing et al. 2022](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/5493/2022/)). Feng et al.'s "data integration" LSTM reached **0.86** the same way ([Feng et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.08949)). Saint-Fleur et al. found 1-day NSE gains of about 8-12% for LSTMs and up to 22% for SAC-SMA from discharge assimilation on CAMELS-US and CAMELS-FR ([HESS 2026](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3497/2026/)). Google's v2 model extends its reliable horizon by **6 days in gauged basins but only 1 day in ungauged ones** ([EGUsphere 2026-2283](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-2283/)). The value of gauge history therefore persists well into the multi-day range the paper targets.

The practical consequence: the GNN sees observed gauge flow up to issue time and forecast precipitation afterwards. It must be compared against at least one baseline in the *same* regime, and every other baseline must carry an explicit regime label. The table below collects the reported numbers. None comes from the Tennessee basin, and the notes found no published LSTM, NWM, GloFAS or differentiable-model forecast skill specific to the Tennessee River basin or the Southeast.

Two things stand out. First, **lead-time-resolved NSE/KGE for NWP-forced, sub-daily ML forecasts at 1-10 days over CONUS barely exists**. Google reports CDFs and event F1, and Hunt et al. report figures at ten western gauges. The paper's per-lead curves will therefore be new reference numbers, not reproductions. Second, simulation-mode numbers above 0.7 cannot be compared with a forecast model's skill at day 7 without stating that the former used observed precipitation.

## The NWM medium-range archive is the only like-for-like operational benchmark

### What it is and why it fits

The NWM is NOAA's operational CONUS hydrologic model, and its channel network *is* NHDPlus v2. The `feature_id` attribute in the output files reads "NHDPlusv2 ComIDs within CONUS", across 2,776,734 reaches ([sample v3.0 file](https://storage.googleapis.com/national-water-model/nwm.20240601/medium_range_blend/nwm.t00z.medium_range_blend.channel_rt.f024.conus.nc); [Cosgrove et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/66536/noaa_66536_DS1.pdf)). The GNN's ~59k Tennessee reaches can therefore be compared reach-for-reach with no crosswalk, a property no other benchmark offers. The medium-range configuration runs **four times daily to 240 h**. Member 1 is forced by the latest GFS and runs 10 days. Time-lagged members run to 8.5 days ([Cosgrove et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/66536/noaa_66536_DS1.pdf)). Its initial conditions come from the Analysis and Assimilation cycle, which "features data assimilation" of USGS streamflow by nudging ([Cosgrove et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/66536/noaa_66536_DS1.pdf)). The NWM forecast therefore occupies the same information regime as the GNN: observed flow up to issue time, NWP precipitation after it. The no-DA parallel runs (`medium_range_no_da`, present from v2.1) offer a built-in ablation that shows how much the NWM gains from gauges ([GCS listing](https://storage.googleapis.com/storage/v1/b/national-water-model/o?delimiter=/&prefix=nwm.20240601/&fields=prefixes)).

### Three versions inside the test window

The test window spans **three model versions**, and results must be stratified by them. v2.0 ran until **2021-04-20**. v2.1 began "on or about April 20, 2021, beginning with the 1200 UTC run" and added "enhanced treatment of reservoirs" ([SCN 20-119](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf2/scn20-119nwm_v2.1_aad.pdf)). v3.0 began "on or about September 20, 2023". It added an NBM-forced deterministic `medium_range_blend`, RFC-supplied reservoir outflow forecasts at 392 locations, a new surface-runoff scheme and an int64 `feature_id` ([SCN 23-76](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2023_24/scn23-76_national_water_model_v3.0_aab.pdf)). Direct bucket listings show the output structure also changed. Under v2.0, `medium_range_mem1` channel output is **3-hourly** (f003-f240, 320 files/day). From v2.1 it is **hourly** (960 files/day). The GFS ensemble drops from **seven members to six** under v3.0, and the blend appears from 2023-10 ([GCS listing](https://storage.googleapis.com/storage/v1/b/national-water-model/o?delimiter=/&prefix=nwm.20240601/&fields=prefixes)). For v2.0 (Jan 2020-Apr 2021), a 6-hourly mean is built from only two 3-hourly instantaneous values, a minor resolution handicap worth noting.

### Getting the data

The Google Cloud bucket is the complete archive. A direct listing found daily folders from **2018-09-17 to 2026-10-12, 2,948 folders and zero missing days** ([GCS listing](https://storage.googleapis.com/storage/v1/b/national-water-model/o?delimiter=/&prefix=nwm.2020&fields=prefixes)). File-level gaps exist, however. A first-of-month sample found every date complete except **2020-04-01 (246 of 320 mem1 files)**, so an exhaustive audit is needed before scoring ([GCS listing](https://storage.googleapis.com/storage/v1/b/national-water-model/o?prefix=nwm.20200401/medium_range_mem1/)). The other routes do not cover 2020-2025. NOMADS holds a 48-hour rolling window ([Sci. Data 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-023-02316-7)). AWS `noaa-nwm-pds` is described as a four-week rolling archive, and a listing shows folders only from 2025-01-01 ([AWS Registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/noaa-nwm-pds/); [S3 listing](https://noaa-nwm-pds.s3.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&delimiter=/&max-keys=1000)). Azure/Planetary Computer also mirrors operational output from 2018 ([Markert et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/66525/noaa_66525_DS1.pdf)). Each NetCDF holds one lead time for all 2.7 million reaches. Pulling only the 00Z cycle at 6-hourly leads for mem1 is roughly 0.6 GB/day, or about 1.3 TB for six years. Byte-range reads via kerchunk, the BigQuery `national_water_model` tables, the Parquet mirror `gs://national-water-model-parq`, or the CIROH REST API at nwm-api.ciroh.org avoid full downloads ([Markert et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/66525/noaa_66525_DS1.pdf)). The BigQuery tables' date coverage was not confirmed. TEEHR is CIROH's open-source framework for exactly this kind of NWM-vs-observation evaluation ([CIROH](https://ciroh.ua.edu/?p=1429)).

Gauge association should be cross-checked, not trusted. The RouteLink files (CSV versions 2.1.6, 2.2.3, 3.0.7 on [HydroShare](https://beta.hydroshare.org/resource/e9fe66730d184bdfbaea19639bd7cb55)) carry NWM's gauge links. USGS GageLoc indexes about 26,000 gauges to NHDPlus v2.1 COMIDs with an explicit warning about location differences ([ScienceBase](https://sciencebase.gov/catalog/item/5c86a747e4b09388244b3da1)). Known mis-associations exist ([hydrotools #206](https://github.com/NOAA-OWP/hydrotools/issues/206)).

### Retrospective runs are a simulation reference, not a forecast

The v2.1 (Feb 1979-Dec 2020) and v3.0 (Feb 1979-Jan 2023) retrospectives on AWS are AORC-forced open-loop runs with **no streamflow DA** ([AWS Registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/nwm-archive/)). They cover only 2020 to January 2023 of the test window. Their best uses are as an "observed-forcing" reference that splits NWM forecast error into model error and forcing error, and as a pre-2020 series for climatology or post-processor training. Scoring a gauge-informed GNN against them at short leads would be an unfair comparison. CAMELSH's Zenodo release already bundles hourly NWM v2.1/v3.0 retrospective series at 8,664 USGS-matched locations ([Zenodo 21302482](https://zenodo.org/records/21302482)), which saves extraction work.

Published medium-range verification is thin. One real-time study covered only July-August 2020 at about 3,000 sites. It found peak bias and timing improved over the first 6 hours "but change little in the hours that follow", with ensemble means scoring higher at days 4-10 ([Cosgrove et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/66536/noaa_66536_DS1.pdf)). An early NWS briefing flagged a **positive medium-range bias in the Southeast RFC** region ([NWS briefing](https://www.weather.gov/media/wrn/calendar/NationalWaterModel.pdf)). The Tennessee-specific open question is reservoirs. Below TVA dams, NWM skill depends on its reservoir treatment, and it was not verified whether any TVA projects are among v3.0's 392 RFC-forecast reservoirs. Regulated and unregulated gauges must be scored separately.

### Other operational archives fail coverage, resolution or independence tests

**GloFAS** has a public forecast archive from **5 November 2019**. It is daily, ensemble-forced, runs to 30 days, and is downloadable from the Copernicus EWDS. Its grid is 0.1° under v3.1 and 0.05° under v4.0 from 2023-07-26 ([EWDS](https://ewds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/cems-glofas-forecast?tab=overview)). Reforecasts run 2003-2022, twice weekly with 11 members ([EWDS reforecast](https://ewds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/cems-glofas-reforecast?tab=overview)). A ~22 km² cell and a daily step make it defensible only at large gauges (on the order of >1,000 km², an inference) after daily aggregation. Its own skill page notes weak KGE in parts of the southern US ([GloFAS skill](https://global-flood.emergency.copernicus.eu/technical-information/glofas-skill)). Downloads are slow, roughly one issue date per request.

**Google Flood Hub** offers real-time forecasts only, through a waitlisted API ([Google Developers](https://developers.google.com/flood-forecasting?hl=en)). The GRRR dataset has reforecasts for 2016-2023 ([Google blog](https://research.google/blog/a-flood-forecasting-ai-model-trained-and-evaluated-globally/)). The Nature 2024 Zenodo archive holds daily reforecasts in mm/day at Caravan/HydroBASINS gauges ([Zenodo 10397664](https://zenodo.org/records/10397664)). At best these give a partial 2020-2023 daily comparison at overlapping gauges.

**RFC river forecasts** are archived as SHEF text by the Iowa Environmental Mesonet. Examples include LMRFC "MAINSTEM_TN" stage forecasts at 6-hourly steps to day 5 with 24-hour QPF, and 9,577 RVFMRX products for 2020-2025 ([IEM](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/cgi-bin/afos/retrieve.py?pil=RVFOHX&sdate=2021-01-01&edate=2021-01-04&limit=1&fmt=text)). These forecasts are stage-based, cover only tens of points, and in this basin are likely coordinated with TVA releases. That conflicts with the paper's no-TVA-forecast constraint, so they should be excluded unless independence can be shown. **HEFS** has no public archive, and **EFAS** is Europe-only ([NOAA IR](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/59892); [ECMWF](https://www.ecmwf.int/en/forecasts/datasets/river-discharge-and-related-forecasted-data-european-flood-awareness-system)).

## A retrained autoregressive forecast LSTM is the baseline reviewers will demand

### NeuralHydrology supplies the architectures but not the forecast data layout

NeuralHydrology (BSD-3, actively maintained) is the de facto reproducible LSTM library. Its latest release, **v1.13.0**, added "AORC hourly forcing support", an xLSTM backbone and early stopping ([release notes](https://github.com/neuralhydrology/neuralhydrology/releases/tag/v1.13.0)). Since v1.8.0 it has four encoder-decoder forecast LSTMs: handoff, multihead, sequential and stacked. Each takes separate `hindcast_inputs` and `forecast_inputs`, a `forecast_seq_length`, and a `timestep_counter` lead-time input ([NH models](https://neuralhydrology.readthedocs.io/en/latest/usage/models.html); [NH config](https://neuralhydrology.readthedocs.io/en/latest/usage/config.html)). The **Handoff-Forecast-LSTM** is closest to Google's v1 operational design. A hindcast LSTM runs to issue time, and a `state_handoff_network` passes its states to a separately weighted forecast LSTM. Observed flow enters through `lagged_features`. `autoregressive_inputs` is documented only for ARLSTM, which is "much slower than CudaLSTM", and it was not confirmed whether the forecast models accept it. Missing gauge data can be handled with `nan_handling_method: masked_mean` plus training-time dropout of inputs ([NH config](https://neuralhydrology.readthedocs.io/en/latest/usage/config.html); [Gauch et al. 2025](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6221/2025/)).

The main obstacle is a documented limitation: `forecast_inputs` "does not currently support a forecast rollout" and is unsupported for multi-timescale models ([NH config](https://neuralhydrology.readthedocs.io/en/latest/usage/config.html)). In practice, core NeuralHydrology indexes forecast inputs by valid time. It cannot natively hold a different NWP precipitation value for the same valid time from each issue time, which a true forecast-mode comparison needs. There are three workarounds. One is training on AORC as the "forecast" input and swapping in NWP at test time, which is common but creates a train/test forcing mismatch. Another is building lead-specific columns per sample. The third is using Google's fork, which was explicitly extended "to support forecast sequences".

### OpenHydroNet is the stronger operational template, with one unverified gap

Google open-sourced its operational framework on **3 June 2026** as google-research/flood-forecasting, documented as OpenHydroNet ([Google blog](https://research.google/blog/the-next-chapter-in-flood-resilience-open-sourcing-googles-hydrology-framework/)). It is an Apache-2.0, "heavily modified fork of NeuralHydrology". It contains the **Mean-Embedding-Forecast-LSTM** (in production since December 2025) and the former production Handoff-Forecast-LSTM. It offers optional gradient-based DA at inference that adjusts embeddings, not weights, to match recent observed flow (`--assimilate`, ME-LSTM only), a `pretrained-models` folder, and Caravan/MultiMet loaders ([repo README](https://github.com/google-research/flood-forecasting/)). The ME-LSTM embeds each forcing product separately and averages the available ones. This is a masked-mean design meant to survive weather-data outages ([EGU26-6973](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-6973.html)). Using it would let the paper say its baseline *is* Google's operational architecture. The catch is that the README never states temporal resolution, and **whether it trains at 6-hourly resolution is unverified**. Google's models, the pretrained weights and the Flood Hub API are all daily and run to 7 days. Comparing against them directly would require aggregating the GNN to daily and truncating at 7 days.

### The closest published template and the other LSTM references

Hunt et al. (2022) is the nearest published analogue to the paper's design. It is an LSTM issuing **6-hourly forecasts out to 10 days (40 steps)**, driven by the ECMWF IFS control member. At 5-day lead in the operational period it beat bias-corrected GloFAS at all ten western-US gauges. However, it used single-gauge models over a short evaluation ([Hunt et al. 2022](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/5449/2022/)). Its 28-step input window and 40-step lead structure can be copied directly. The single-basin training should not be, given the "never train an LSTM on a single basin" consensus ([Kratzert et al. 2024](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/)).

Three other LSTM references serve as context, not primary baselines. **MTS-LSTM** (Gauch 2021) is the most-cited hourly CAMELS number. It is a simulation model, though, and its released predictions on Zenodo cover only 2008-2018 ([HESS 2021](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/)). It can be retrained in perfect-forcing mode as an upper-bound-forcing reference. The **NextGen BMI LSTM** (NOAA-OWP/lstm, CIROH-UA/lstm) is the US operational-framework LSTM. It runs hourly on AORC with CAMELS-trained weights and uses no observed flow ([CIROH-UA/lstm](https://github.com/CIROH-UA/lstm)). Coupled with t-route it scores only **0.359** median hourly NSE across CONUS ([Yang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06794)), which makes it an honest "operational ML floor". **Frame-style NWM post-processing LSTMs** lifted daily CAMELS NSE from 0.62 to 0.73-0.75 ([Frame et al. 2021](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/53162)). An LSTM that corrects NWM medium-range output at gauges is cheap to build from the retrospective and forecast archives. It answers the reviewer question of whether a corrected NWM would have been enough.

### Recipe for the fair LSTM baseline

The baseline should be one **regional** model across all evaluation gauges, not one per gauge. The training gauge set should match the GNN's (the notes reference 34 TVA plus 94 CAMELS-H gauges). Inputs should be 6-hourly area-averaged AORC over each gauge's NHDPlus-delineated upstream area, with lagged observed flow plus a 0/1 availability flag as hindcast-only inputs. Following Nearing 2022, lagged values should be randomly withheld during training so the model tolerates gauge outages. The forecast inputs should be the *same* NWP precipitation product the GNN uses, plus a lead counter, with `forecast_seq_length` = 40 and roughly a one-year hindcast. Training should use an NSE loss (or a CMAL head for probabilistic output), the identical 1979-2018 / 2019 / 2020-2025 splits, and a 3-8-seed ensemble, as in Kratzert 2019 and Nearing 2024. The two variants worth running are a handoff forecast LSTM and a sequential forecast LSTM. A plain CudaLSTM simulation model (no observed flow, AORC forcing) serves as the floor. No published example combines lagged observed flow and real NWP at sub-daily resolution in NeuralHydrology, so the configuration must be described in full. That 6-hourly `generic` datasets run cleanly in the forecast models also remains to be tested.

## Graph and differentiable models are positioning comparators, not drop-in baselines

### Published GNNs are small, short-lead or simulation-only

The GNN literature splits into regimes that do not overlap with this paper. **Gauge-graph forecasters with observed discharge** forecast hours ahead. Kirschstein & Sun used 24 h of history for a 6 h lead on 358 LamaH-CE gauges ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/kirschstein24a.html)). Wang et al. covered 1-24 h leads on the same gauges, with MIT-licensed code ([arXiv 2410.05536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05536)). **Multi-day graph routers run as simulations without observed flow.** The LSTM-GAT router on 530 Danube subbasins reached mean NSE 0.61 ([Mosaffa et al. 2026](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/2079/2026/)). RiverGraphNet routed Noah-MP runoff on a NextGen-hydrofabric graph at 23 Arizona gauges, with median KGE skill ~0.72 vs ~0 for RAPID ([EGUsphere 2026-4157](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4157/)). The physics-guided reach graphs (Sun et al. 2022 on 552 NHDPlus reaches; USGS river-dl in the Delaware) are surrogates or fusion models ([HESS 2022](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/5163/2022/); [river-dl](https://api.github.com/repos/USGS-R/river-dl)). Google's graph-routing program appears only in EGU abstracts (2021, EGU24-8899), with no paper or code ([EGU24-8899](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU24/EGU24-8899.html)). Its open v2 model has no graph component ([EGUsphere 2026-2283](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-2283/)).

Licensing narrows the options further. The most comparable GNN-routing repositories (Kirschstein's neural-flood-forecasting, Mosaffa's GNN_flow_routing, RiverGraphNet) **carry no licence file**, so reuse needs author permission. Permissive options are FloodGNNs (MIT), HydroGAT (Apache-2.0, pixel graph), ddr (Apache-2.0) and river-dl (CC0, archived) ([GitHub API licence checks](https://api.github.com/repos/Dreamzz5/FloodGNNs)). None of the message-passing routers has been shown beyond about 550 nodes. At ~59k reaches, only physics-embedded differentiable routers (180k-800k reaches) and gridded global models (RiverMamba at 0.05° to 7 days; GraphRiverCast at 0.25°) operate at comparable or larger scale ([arXiv 2505.22535](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22535); [arXiv 2602.22293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22293)).

### Topology evidence is mixed, which makes ablations mandatory

The most rigorous negative result is Kirschstein & Sun. Across six adjacency definitions, three edge orientations and three architectures, an **isolated-node GNN and an MLP matched the best topology-aware GNN** (85.37% vs 85.56% weighted NSE). Learned edge weights correlated weakly with physical ones ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)). Wang et al. attribute this to over-squashing on tree-like river graphs. They report that a dense reachability graph consistently beat both topological and isolated variants and EA-LSTM, with a ~10-hour lead advantage ([arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.05536v3)). Mosaffa et al. found gains concentrated at downstream, highly connected stations ([HESS 2026](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/2079/2026/)). Taken together, topology adds little when a gauge's own recent flow is known and the lead is short. It matters more as lead time exceeds local travel times, at large downstream basins, at ungauged reaches and for spike floods. The paper's whole-upstream aggregation with travel-time kernels directly addresses over-squashing. It still has to show *where* topology helps, through an isolated-node variant of the same architecture and through skill stratified by lead and drainage area. No study has tested topology at reach scale with observed flow and multi-day leads, or in a heavily regulated system like the TVA network.

### δHBV2.0MTS-MC is the strongest physics-ML comparator, with caveats

The hourly differentiable model δHBV2.0MTS-MC is the best-matched physics-informed comparator. It runs on the NextGen hydrofabric (>800,000 unit basins) with AORC forcing and end-to-end Muskingum-Cunge routing. It scored median hourly NSE **0.683** across 2,831 CONUS gauges, and **0.729 in humid basins**, against 0.461 for NWM3.0. It cut peak-timing errors from 7-8 h to 4.5-6 h ([Yang et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06794)). This is a preprint, and the gains over NWM were concentrated in the arid West and Great Plains. On CAMELS it roughly ties MTS-LSTM (0.710 vs 0.726 NSE) while reducing high-flow bias. Its cost is low at inference (10 years hourly over all CONUS in about 4 GPU-hours) and high in training (about 5 A100-days for CONUS). It ships as a BMI module (`dhbv2` on PyPI) under a **non-commercial PSU licence** ([dhbv2](https://github.com/mhpi/dhbv2)).

There are three Tennessee-specific problems. **Training excluded gauges above 50,000 km²**, the model has **no reservoir operations**, and **whether pretrained weights are publicly downloadable was not confirmed**. It has also never been run with NWP forcing. The only forecast-like δ evidence is variational DA on CAMELS, which raised 1-day median NSE from 0.75 to 0.82, on par with LSTM+DA ([Jamaat et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16444)). A δ forecast baseline would therefore be new work. It would mean warming up through issue time with AORC (358 days), driving the next 10 days with the same NWP forcing, and optionally adjusting states at t0. Scoring would happen at gauges, since the hydrofabric differs from NHDPlus v2. The lighter alternative is to run it in simulation mode for 2020-2025 as an out-of-sample "best physics-ML with observed forcing" reference, restricted to unregulated gauges. `ddr` (Apache-2.0) provides the differentiable routing on hydrofabric v2.2 and MERIT but **does not support NHDPlus v2** ([ddr](https://github.com/deepgroundwater/ddr)).

## Datasets, forcing archives and metrics fix the playing field

### CAMELSH supplies the gauges; forcing archives need care

The "CAMELS-H" in the brief is **CAMELSH** (Tran et al., *Scientific Data* 2025). It covers 9,008 GAGES-II basins, **3,166 with observed hourly USGS flow**, for 1980-2024, with NLDAS-2 and ERA5-Land forcing and 600+ attributes including regulation indicators ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12304116/)). Its streamflow is NWIS instantaneous values aggregated to hourly in UTC with qualifier flags kept and no further QC. Because the dataset ends on **31 December 2024**, 2025 observations must be pulled from NWIS IV with identical aggregation ([Zenodo 21302482](https://zenodo.org/records/21302482)). It ships no network topology, so the NHDPlus edge list and gauge-to-COMID indexing are the authors' responsibility. LamaH-CE is the one topology-aware large-sample dataset and the de facto GNN benchmark ([ESSD](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/13/4529/2021/essd-13-4529-2021-discussion.html)). A secondary run there would make the method's generality concrete.

For forecast precipitation, all main NOAA products for 2020-2025 sit on AWS NODD and are readable with Herbie. GEFS is available from 2017, with a layout change at the **GEFSv12 upgrade on 2020-09-23**. HRRR is available from 2014 and NBM from 2020-05-18. The GFS bucket, however, **starts only on 2021-01-01**, so 2020 GFS must come from NCEI or NCAR RDA ([S3 listings](https://noaa-gfs-bdp-pds.s3.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&delimiter=/&prefix=gfs.&max-keys=2); [Herbie](https://pypi.python.org/pypi/herbie-data)). The GEFSv12 reforecast (2000-2019, 5 daily members) can supply forecast-precipitation training data inside the 1979-2018 period ([AWS Registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/noaa-gefs-reforecast/)). The cleanest single choice is the NWM bucket's own `forcing_medium_range/` folders, which hold the exact downscaled GFS forcing NWM used throughout 2020-2025, with `forcing_medium_range_blend/` (NBM) from v3.0 ([GCS listing](https://storage.googleapis.com/storage/v1/b/national-water-model/o?delimiter=/&prefix=nwm.20240601/&fields=prefixes)). Driving the GNN and the LSTM baseline with that forcing makes the NWM comparison a pure hydrologic-model comparison, with forcing held fixed.

### Metrics follow the NeuralHydrology and Nearing 2024 conventions

The community metric set is NeuralHydrology's. It comprises NSE, KGE with its r/α/β components, α-NSE and β-NSE, FHV (top 2%), FLV, peak timing (window 12 for hourly), missed peaks, peak MAPE, and for probabilistic output CRPS, PICP and MPIW ([NH metrics](https://neuralhydrology.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/neuralhydrology.evaluation.metrics.html)). Flood skill should follow Nearing et al. 2024. Return-period thresholds are computed per gauge, separately for observed and modelled series. An event counts as a hit when both cross the threshold within **two days**, and precision, recall and F1 are reported at 1-10-year return periods ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10954541/)). For US gauges the current standard is **Bulletin 17C** (log-Pearson III with EMA and Multiple Grubbs-Beck, implemented in PeakFQ), not the 17B that Nearing cited ([USGS TM 4-B5](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/tm4B5)). Thresholds must be fit on training-period annual maxima only. A "1-year" event needs an explicit definition (e.g., Q1.01 or partial duration). KGE needs care in interpretation: a mean-flow prediction scores **KGE = −0.41, not 0** ([Knoben et al. 2019](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/23/4323/2019/)).

Benchmark choice drives apparent skill. In EFAS, long-term-average benchmarks were "very easily beaten" and produced naïve skill. Meteorological persistence (driving the model with the latest observed weather) was the most useful benchmark ([Pappenberger et al. 2015](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/39072/)). Streamflow persistence is a known hard-to-beat reference at short leads ([arXiv 2106.09547](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.09547)). For statistics, the Google/JKU precedent is paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across gauges with Cohen's d. Gauch used α = 0.001 with no multiple-comparison correction ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2010.07921); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10954541/)). Nearing 2024 is also the model for disclosing an asymmetric benchmark: it reported that GloFAS was scored only at 0-day lead and was partly calibrated on the test gauges.

## The recommended benchmark suite and protocol

The suite below is ordered by priority. Tiers 1-2 are required for the main claims. Tier 3 strengthens positioning. Tier 4 is supplementary.

| Tier | Benchmark | Regime | How to obtain/run for TN 2020-2025 | Scored at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Streamflow persistence (last observed 6-h mean at issue time) | Observed Q | Computed from gauge data | Gauges |
| 1 | Day-of-year 6-hourly climatology (1979-2018) | None | Computed from training-period observations | Gauges |
| 1 | NWM medium-range mem1 (10 d), plus GFS ensemble mean (8.5 d) and v3.0 blend | NWP + USGS DA | `gs://national-water-model` channel_rt, 00Z (ideally all 4 cycles), averaged to 6 h | All ~59k COMIDs and gauges |
| 1 | NWM `medium_range_no_da` (v2.1+) | NWP, no DA | Same bucket | COMIDs and gauges |
| 2 | Regional AR forecast LSTM (NH handoff/sequential, or OpenHydroNet ME-LSTM if 6-hourly works) | Observed Q + same NWP | Retrained on the GNN's gauges, forcing and splits; 3-8 seeds | Gauges |
| 2 | GNN isolated-node ablation (edges removed) | Same as GNN | Same code, no message passing | All reaches and gauges |
| 2 | GNN with zero future precipitation and with AORC "perfect" future precipitation | Bracketing | Same model, swapped lead-window input | All reaches and gauges |
| 3 | Simulation LSTM (CudaLSTM/MTS-style, AORC, no Q) | Simulation | NeuralHydrology, same splits | Gauges |
| 3 | NWM v3.0 retrospective (to Jan 2023) | Simulation, AORC | AWS `noaa-nwm-retrospective-3-0-pds` or CAMELSH bundle | COMIDs and gauges |
| 3 | δHBV2.0MTS-MC simulation (forecast-mode optional) | Simulation (or new forecast mode) | `dhbv2` / δMG on hydrofabric VPU 06 with AORC; weights to be confirmed | Unregulated gauges |
| 3 | NWM + LSTM post-processor (Frame-style) | NWP + NWM output + Q | Train on retrospective, apply to medium-range | Gauges |
| 4 | NextGen LSTM-TRoute | Simulation | NOAA-OWP/lstm + t-route via NGIAB | Gauges |
| 4 | GloFAS v3.1/v4.0 forecasts | NWP ensemble | EWDS area subsets, daily | Large gauges, daily |
| 4 | Google GRRR / Nature 2024 reforecasts | NWP, daily | Colab / Zenodo, 2020-2023 overlap | Overlapping gauges, daily |

Run the protocol on **matched samples**: identical issue times (00Z at minimum, ideally all four cycles to match NWM), identical gauges, and identical 6-hourly valid times. Drop any step where the observation or any model is missing. Score every metric **per lead step (1-40) and summarised by day**. Report median and mean across gauges with bootstrap confidence intervals, resampling gauges and blocking by year to respect temporal dependence. The core metrics are NSE, KGE and its components, the persistence-referenced NSE skill score (which separates real forecasting skill from autocorrelation), FHV, peak-timing error with a ±2-step (±12 h) window, and peak MAPE. Event metrics are POD, FAR, CSI and F1 at Bulletin-17C Q1.01/Q2/Q5 thresholds with a ±2-day window. Few 5-year events will occur in six years, so report pooled CSI alongside per-gauge medians. If the GNN is probabilistic, add CRPS/CRPSS against persistence and climatology ensembles.

**Stratify** every headline result four ways: regulated vs reference gauges (GAGES-II flags, plus explicit below-TVA-dam status); drainage-area classes; the three NWM version periods (to 2021-04-20, to 2023-09-20, after); and rising vs falling limb or high vs low flow. **Test** pairwise against each benchmark with Wilcoxon signed-rank and Cohen's d, correcting across leads and baselines with Holm or Benjamini-Hochberg. **Document** robustness by withholding lagged observations at random gauges at inference. **Prove** no leakage, meaning no observed flow after issue time and thresholds fit only on training years. For any "ungauged reach" claim, add a spatial hold-out of gauges in hydrologically separated sub-basins.

## Conclusion

The NWM medium-range archive is more valuable here than its reputation suggests. It is the only public system that forecasts at the paper's exact NHDPlus v2 reaches, already assimilates USGS gauges, archives all of 2020-2025 at 10-day lead, and publishes its own forcing. That allows a rare forcing-controlled comparison in which an operational process model and a learned graph model see the same weather and the same gauges. The decisive comparison, though, will not be against NWM, which simulation studies already place well below modern ML. It will be against a retrained autoregressive LSTM and the GNN's own isolated-node ablation, because the strongest published GNN study found that topology adds nothing over a per-gauge model when recent flow is known. The paper's distinctive contribution is to show at which leads, basin sizes and regulation regimes the river network starts to matter. Its structure gives it a credible mechanism for that, since whole-upstream travel-time aggregation sidesteps the over-squashing that undermined earlier GNNs.

Three uncertainties should shape the work plan. It is not yet known whether OpenHydroNet trains at 6-hourly resolution, whether δHBV2.0MTS weights are downloadable, or how NWM handles TVA reservoirs. Each could move a baseline between tiers, and all three can be checked in days, before any training begins. Regulation is also the threat most likely to dominate results. Every comparator, learned or physical, was developed on minimally disturbed basins, so the regulated/unregulated stratification is not optional. It is where the paper will either show that a graph model learns reservoir-mediated dynamics or show that no public model, including this one, yet does.
